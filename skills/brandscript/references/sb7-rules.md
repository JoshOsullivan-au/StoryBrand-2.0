# SB7 rules by section

Paraphrased from Miller, *Building a StoryBrand* (HarperCollins, 2017), called 1e below, and *Building a StoryBrand 2.0* (HarperCollins Leadership, 2025), called 2.0. Chapters 1 to 11 have the same numbers in both editions. Chapter 12 in 2.0 is new. No text is quoted. `[script]` marks a rule that `brandscript.py check` enforces. `[judgement]` marks a rule the skill applies. `(2.0)` marks a rule that is new or changed in 2.0. Where the editions differ, 2.0 wins.

2.0 frames each BrandScript section as a **sound bite**: a short line the brand repeats word for word until customers can say it back.

## Why the framework works (chapters 1 to 3)

- People scan everything for what helps them survive and thrive. Information that does not help them is ignored.
- Processing information costs effort. Confusing copy makes people stop listening to save that effort. "If you confuse, you lose."
- Story is a sense-making device. It organises information so the reader does not have to.
- The seven-part plot: a **character** who wants something has a **problem**, meets a **guide** who gives them a **plan** and **calls them to action**, which helps them avoid **failure** and ends in **success**.
- The grunt test: within five seconds, a visitor can say what you offer, how it makes life better, and what to do to buy it. (2.0) The same test applies to ads, lead generators and emails. Don't be clever, be clear. See `[[grunt-test]]`.
- Story clarity: at any point, a reader can say what the hero wants, who or what opposes them, and what life looks like if they win or lose.
- (2.0) A few sound bites can move revenue more than an ad campaign or a style guide. 2.0's example is a three-word packaging line that tied a product to a family survival need (children's delight) and lifted sales in a test market.
- Build the brand script first, then one per division, then one per product, then one per segment if needed.

## 1. Character (chapter 4)

Principle: the customer is the hero, not the brand.

- `[script]` Name the customer and one want.
- `[script]` (warn) The want is a single desire. Lists joined by "and", commas or slashes are flagged.
- `[judgement]` The want opens a story gap: a distance between the customer and what they want.
- `[judgement]` The want ties to survival. Categories from the book: conserve money, save time, build a social network, gain status, gather resources, the desire to be generous, the desire for meaning.
- `[judgement]` Vague aspirations fail. A clear, concrete destination beats a clever tagline.

Brainstorm prompts:
- Where does the customer want to get to, in their words?
- Which survival category does that destination serve?
- Could a stranger repeat it back after one hearing?

## 2. Has a problem (chapter 5)

Principle: companies sell solutions to external problems. People buy solutions to internal problems. (2.0) Chapter 3 states it more bluntly: people buy only solutions to problems, so a brand that does not name the problem clearly sells nothing.

- `[script]` Define villain, external, internal and philosophical problem.
- `[script]` (warn) One villain only.
- `[script]` (warn) The philosophical problem uses should, shouldn't or deserve.
- `[judgement]` The villain is the root cause, not a feeling. It is relatable, singular and real. It can be a thing or an idea given a personality.
- `[judgement]` (2.0) The villain does not have to be serious. A playful rival works where the audience enjoys the contest.
- `[judgement]` External: the tangible problem. Internal: the frustration or self-doubt the external problem causes. Philosophical: why it is wrong that anyone should suffer this.
- `[judgement]` A product framed as solving all three levels at once carries more perceived value. The buy button is the action that resolves all three.

Brainstorm prompts:
- What is the one root cause your product fights?
- What tangible problem does it cause?
- How does that make the customer feel about themselves?
- Why is it unfair that they put up with it?

## 3. Meets a guide (chapter 6)

Principle: customers are not looking for another hero. They are looking for a guide.

- `[script]` One empathy statement.
- `[script]` At least one authority item, typed as testimonial, statistic, award or logo.
- `[script]` (warn) More than three testimonials. Short ones work best.
- `[judgement]` A brand that plays the hero competes with the customer for attention and loses.
- `[judgement]` Empathy answers "can I trust you". Authority answers "can I respect you". Both are needed.
- `[judgement]` Empathy speaks to the internal problem. Common openings: "we understand how...", "no one should have to...".
- `[judgement]` Authority shows competence without boasting. Let others speak for you.
- `[judgement]` (2.0) The guide does not transform. A guide with its own doubts or arc confuses the audience about who the hero is. The guide stays confident so the spotlight stays on the hero.

## 4. Who gives them a plan (chapter 7)

Principle: customers trust a guide who has a plan. (2.0) The plan comes as baby steps.

- `[judgement]` (2.0) The plan relieves cognitive dissonance: the doubt a customer feels just before ordering. A few simple steps carry them from not knowing to knowing.

- `[script]` Process plan of 3 to 6 steps. More than six becomes phases.
- `[script]` (warn) More than four steps reads slower.
- `[script]` (warn) Plans have a title.
- `[judgement]` A process plan removes confusion. It can cover steps before purchase, after purchase, or both.
- `[judgement]` An agreement plan removes fear. List every fear the customer has about the industry, then answer each with a promise. It often sits in the background.
- `[judgement]` Naming the plan raises its perceived value.

## 5. And calls them to action (chapter 8)

Principle: customers do not act unless they are challenged to act.

- `[script]` One direct call to action.
- `[script]` (warn) One transitional call to action.
- `[judgement]` Direct: buy, book, call, schedule, register. It leads to a sale. Repeat the same wording everywhere.
- `[judgement]` Transitional: free information, testimonials, samples, free trials. It stakes a claim on the field, builds reciprocity and positions the brand as guide.
- `[judgement]` A soft or hidden call to action reads as doubt in the product.

## 6. That helps them avoid failure (chapter 9)

Principle: every person is trying to avoid a tragic ending.

- `[script]` At least three failure items.
- `[script]` (warn) Each of the three kinds is present: practical consequence, emotional consequence, missed opportunity. The book lists money, health, opportunity cost and quality of life as the questions to ask.
- `[script]` (warn) Fewer failure items than success items.
- `[judgement]` If nothing is at stake, there is no story. Loss aversion is a stronger motivator than gain.
- `[judgement]` Fear is salt. Moderate fear changes behaviour. Too much makes people block the message.
- `[judgement]` The four-step fear appeal: show the reader is vulnerable, say they should act, name the specific action, call them to take it.

## 7. And ends in success (chapter 10)

Principle: never assume people understand how the brand changes their life. Tell them.

- `[script]` At least one success item.
- `[script]` (warn) Both kinds are present: practical results and how they feel.
- `[judgement]` Be specific. A blurred vision does not move anyone.
- `[judgement]` List the resolution of the external, internal and philosophical problems. The climactic scene closes all three at once.
- `[judgement]` Before and after grid (credited to Ryan Deiss): for the customer before and after the brand, ask what they have, what they feel, what an average day is like, and what their status is.
- `[judgement]` Stories close in three ways: winning power or position (status), union with something that makes the hero whole (relief, less workload, more time), or reaching potential and self-acceptance (inspiration, acceptance, transcendence).
- `[judgement]` Show it in images: people having a good experience with the product.

## Identity transformation (chapter 11)

Principle: people want the brand to take part in their transformation.

- `[script]` Fill both the from and to lines.
- `[judgement]` The to line answers: how does the customer want others to describe them?
- `[judgement]` The from line is the opposite of the to line.
- `[judgement]` The guide's last job is to affirm the change. Heroes do not see their own transformation. Plan a moment that tells the customer how far they have come.
- `[judgement]` (2.0) Write it as "We help our customers go from X to Y."
- `[judgement]` (2.0) An aspirational identity also gives the brand's own team meaning in its work.

## Controlling idea (2.0, chapter 12)

Principle: a good campaign begins and ends with a controlling idea. It answers "what is this story about?".

- `[script]` State one controlling idea.
- `[script]` (warn) Over 20 words. State it simply.
- `[judgement]` Find it in the finished BrandScript: the moral of the story, the main difference from competitors, the one idea that clarifies the offer fastest, the line you want customers to repeat to friends.
- `[judgement]` It is a complete idea and gets a good reaction without explanation. A shortened form is fine once the full form has been said often enough.
- `[judgement]` Submit all copy to it. It sits in the website header and repeats in each section, in the lead generator title, in every follow-up email, on the about page and in testimonials. Messaging is a memorisation exercise.
- `[judgement]` One story, one plot. A second controlling idea is a second campaign.

## One-liner (2.0 chapter 15 step two; 1e Marketing Roadmap task one)

(2.0) Three parts: the problem, the product as solution, the result. Lead with the problem. 1e used four parts: character, problem, plan, success. See `[[one-liner]]`.

## Campaign (2.0 chapters 13 and 15)

Create the sound bites, edit them until they get the reaction (people ask how to buy), then repeat them until the public has them memorised. Seven pieces in order: BrandScript, one-liner, landing page, lead generator, email or text drip, transformation stories, referral system. See `[[messaging-campaign]]`.

## Workshop prompts (implementation workbook)

Extra prompts from the independent implementation workbook (`assets/implementation-workbook.md`). They are not from either book. They help gather evidence for the book rules above.

| Section | Prompts |
|---|---|
| Character | Who uses the offer, who pays, and who influences the decision? What event creates the need? Which audiences are left out of this version? What are the customer's own words for the outcome? Why does it matter now? |
| Problem | What has the customer already tried, and why did it fall short? Which emotional words have customers actually used? What wording names the obstacle without blaming the customer? |
| Guide | For each authority item: is it verified, do we have permission, and where will it appear? What context or limits must go with it? Which claims have no support and must go? What empathy wording would sound patronising? |
| Plan | For each agreement: what concern does it answer, what are its conditions, what proves it can be delivered, and who owns it? |
| Call to action | What exact button wording, where does it go, what does the customer give, and what happens straight after? |
| Failure | What evidence shows each consequence is plausible? What uncertainty must be stated? Who could be harmed or blamed by the wording? What practical next step follows the stakes? |
| Success | For each feature: what does it enable, and why does the customer care? How will the customer recognise progress and completion? |
| Identity | What milestone marks the change, what evidence shows it, and who tells the customer (the guide's affirmation)? |
| Controlling idea | Draft three versions. For each: what does it say, what needs explaining? What does the idea leave out on purpose? What evidence backs its promise? Can the team repeat it word for word? |
